Palette generator

OKLCH · 11 steps · runs locally

Click a swatch to copy its hex. The ramp updates as you type; the URL keeps the base colour and token name.

Why OKLCH instead of HSL

HSL lightness is not perceptual. Two HSL colours with the same L can look wildly different in brightness — pure yellow at 50% is far lighter than pure blue at 50%. A ramp built in HSL produces uneven steps and a muddy middle.

OKLCH splits a colour into perceptual lightness (L), chroma (C) and hue (h). Changing only L keeps the hue and the "colourfulness" feeling constant, which is exactly what a design-system ramp needs.

  • Hue is held constant — the 11 steps are one colour family, not a gradient that drifts toward grey or purple.
  • Chroma tapers at the ends — near white and near black there is less room for saturation. Each step's chroma is scaled down as it moves away from the base lightness, so 50 and 950 do not turn into muddy pastels.
  • Out-of-gamut colours lose chroma, not hue — if a step cannot be shown in sRGB, the generator reduces chroma until it fits instead of clipping a channel and shifting the hue.

Using the ramp in a file

  1. Pick the base. Start from your brand colour rather than a mid-grey; the generator will place it near its natural lightness in the ramp.
  2. Copy the output. CSS for a stylesheet, Tailwind for a config, SCSS for a legacy build, JSON for a token pipeline.
  3. Name the steps, not the jobs. brand-600 survives a redesign; brand-button does not. Semantic names belong in a second layer that points at ramp steps.
  4. Check the pairs you will ship. A ramp is not an accessibility pass. Run the text/background pairs through the contrast checker before you commit tokens.

In FigCraft, local styles and variables take these values directly: one variable per step, modes for light and dark. The app is early alpha, so verify that variables round-trip through export before you depend on it for a team library.

What the generator does not decide

  • Semantic coloursSuccess, warning and danger need their own hues. Deriving them from the brand ramp produces reds that read as brown and greens that read as teal.
  • Dark-mode inversionA 950 step is not automatically the dark-mode version of 50. Dark themes usually need their own lightness targets and slightly lower chroma.
  • Contrast guaranteesSteps are perceptual, not compliance-checked. 600-on-50 passes far more often than 500-on-100, but the pair is what counts.
  • Print and wide-gamutThe output is sRGB hex. P3 or CMYK work needs a colour-managed pipeline.

Questions this page answers

Straight answers, with the limits stated. Every claim here is checkable against the sources linked above.

Why eleven steps and not five?

Eleven is enough to cover surfaces, borders, text and accents without inventing one-off shades, and it matches the 50–950 convention most Tailwind-based systems already use. Fewer steps forces compromises at the extremes; more steps invites near-duplicates.

Does the base colour appear exactly in the ramp?

Not always. The base sets the hue and the chroma scale, and the nearest step is usually within a few percent of it. If you need the exact brand hex, keep it as a separate token and treat the ramp as the scale around it.

Can I get the same ramp in Figma or Penpot?

Yes — copy the hex list. The output is plain sRGB, so it pastes into any tool. In FigCraft the values map onto local styles and variables; in Penpot they map onto colour assets.

Why do two steps look closer than others?

Perceptual lightness is not linear in hex values. The 50–200 range covers a small numeric span but a large visual one near white. The steps are even in OKLCH L, not in RGB.
